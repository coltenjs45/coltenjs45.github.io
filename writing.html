A reflective journey: my cumulative experience at Iowa State

Cumulative reflection

Over my time at Iowa State University, I have had the opportunity to learn countless hands-on technical skills and prepare myself for tasks that my cybersecurity career may bring. These skills include penetration testing, network architecture, and several programming languages. But one thing that Iowa State does differently than other universities is its emphasis on developing skills beyond the technical. These skills include problem-solving when an answer isn't explicitly given, developing solutions with peers, communicating that work through technical documents and orally, and seeing solutions through a wider lens than just engineering. Beyond the classroom, I have continued to build on this foundation through a cybersecurity internship last summer and by earning my Security+ certification, both of which pushed me to apply and expand my knowledge independently. As I look back on my time at Iowa State, there are specific classes that I believe have helped me in each of these categories. A specific course that taught me to look at solutions to engineering problems from a societal, economic, and global impact perspective was my “Legal, Professional and Ethical Issues in Cyber Systems” course. This class followed the societal, ethical, and economic impacts that past developments in technology had pushed the boundaries on. A course that I believed helped me develop my problem-solving skills and pushed me to dive into information beyond what was taught in class was my “App Development” course.

Beyond my coursework during the school year, a big part of my professional and technical growth was due to my internship at Giesecke+Devrient as an IT Security Intern. While I came into the role confident in the technical abilities I developed during labs at Iowa State, I quickly found out that one of my biggest challenges was communicating the need for good security practices to other coworkers who didn't have a technical background. Explaining why a policy mattered in terms that made sense to someone without a cybersecurity vocabulary was a skill I was not exposed to before and was struggling with. To bridge that gap, I overheard a Help Desk coworker explaining how to map a network drive to another employee and explaining why it was important with an analogy of a file cabinet. He explained that a network mapped drive is like a filing cabinet. Only the people with the right key can open certain drawers, and if something happens to one person’s personal cabinet, a copy of the important files will still be safely stored in the shared cabinet for everyone. Seeing my coworker use analogies to explain technical concepts successfully taught me to adopt the same technique. After adopting the technique, my communication with my coworkers about technical concepts was a lot smoother and more efficient. This internship has taught me that there is still a lot to learn outside of my classwork. To keep pursuing that kind of growth on my own, I also earned my CompTIA Security+ certification that same summer. I recognized how competitive the cybersecurity field has become, and I wanted a way to set myself apart when applying for jobs and internships.

A course that really opened my eyes to the impact of engineering decisions was "Legal, Professional and Ethical Issues in Cyber Systems." This class examined real-world cases where cybersecurity intersected with societal and global consequences. One specific case we studied that I remember deeply was Edward Snowden's NSA disclosures. Edward Snowden revealed the extent of government surveillance by the NSA. The whistleblowing executed by Snowden sparked debate over privacy versus national security and showed me that decisions made by engineers daily can carry consequences that reach far beyond just their one organization. Beyond this case, the course also covered common industry regulations such as NIST, PCI, and HIPAA. Learning about these regulations in both class and in a corporate setting taught me that companies have to constantly weigh the cost of compliance against the financial and reputational damage of a breach or violation. These two separate experiences taught me that engineering and cybersecurity do not exist only internally but have societal, global, and economic weight in the real world.

Lastly, a course that pushed me to develop my technical problem-solving and my ability to seek outside knowledge was my “App Development” course. In this course, I worked with a group of four peers to design an Android app from scratch where you could play multiple different card games on a designated server. Early in the semester, we ran into several challenges we hadn't been taught how to solve directly. Some of these challenges included navigating Git, setting up sockets, and learning how to use the Spring Boot framework. Because these topics were not explained in class, my groupmates and I relied heavily on outside example code samples we could find online and on each other to learn the new skills. Outside of technical issues, we also ran into team management issues, as we had two separate teams trying to make a cohesive project (frontend + backend). Because of our split structure, the two teams often felt disjointed. To fix this, my groupmate and I set up a recurring weekly meeting to review what needed to be completed for the upcoming week and communicate progress with the rest of the team. This gave our team clearer, more manageable goals to focus on every week, leading to a lot of success, and we ended up receiving an award for best project of the semester.

Looking back on my time at Iowa State, I have grown as an engineer both inside and outside of my coursework. From internships and certifications to the technical and communication skills I developed in classes like App Development and Legal, Professional and Ethical Issues in Cyber Systems. If I could go back and do anything differently, I would have gotten more involved in cybersecurity clubs and organizations. I think that I missed out on additional hands-on learning opportunities along with the chance to build a stronger connection with my classmates. As for my life outside of graduation, I plan to find a position in cybersecurity and continue earning certifications that prove my proficiency in the field.

General education reflection

Introduction to Music Listening, History of the American City, and Microeconomics

During my time at Iowa State alongside my engineering coursework, I was also granted the opportunity to take multiple non-engineering courses that have broadened the horizons of my learning and opened my eyes to different cultures, history, economics, and how innovation can affect the world in more ways than just providing a solution to a set problem. General education classes that I have taken at Iowa State are “Introduction to Music Listening”, “History of the American City,” and “Microeconomics”. Each of these changed how I think about what it means to solve a problem.

In Introduction to Music Listening, we followed the evolution of music from the 1600s to the present day. What really stood out to me in that class is how the evolution of different types of music was directly related to the engineering of different instruments and techniques for writing and reading music. For example, the invention of the piano was a huge advancement in the world of music. In the 1700s in Italy, Bartolomeo Cristofori created the piano, which allowed players to play many notes at once and play both soft (piano) and loud (forte), which is where its original name, pianoforte, comes from. As manufacturing made pianos more affordable, access to music spread across social classes and allowed for home music-making, which allowed for a larger variety of music styles. This example taught me that engineering advancements can reach far beyond the problem it was built to solve. A better instrument changed how music was written, where it was played, and who could take part in it. As a future engineer, this reminds me to ask what possible effects a design might have on people and culture, and not only whether it works to solve a desired problem.

In History of the American City, the class and I examined how the cities developed from the colonial period to today. This included road layouts, technology, regulation, funding, building style, and social categories like class, race, and gender. The most important lesson I took from the course is that decisions made in the past can shape communities for generations, and that engineering and planning choices carry real consequences for the people who live with them. A clear example of this is the discriminatory housing policy of the 20th century. In the 1930s federal agencies, specifically the federal Home Owners' Loan Corporation, drew maps that labeled a majority of Black neighborhoods as “hazardous” for lending in a process known as redlining. Many residents in these areas were denied the loans that allowed white families to buy homes and build wealth. Along with this, many highways and urban renewal projects were built and routed through these same Black neighborhoods, displacing families and dividing communities. The effect of these decisions and unfair policies did not end when the policies did. Because of the lack of home loans for people in the “redlined” areas, homeownership was delayed for many Black families, making them unable to build generational wealth and leading to a racial wealth gap that is still seen in cities today. This taught me that although I am not an infrastructure policymaker as an engineer, my decisions can impact people beyond my lifetime, and as a future engineer, I need to ask who benefits from a project, who could be harmed, and what effects my solutions can have on future generations.

In my Microeconomics class, I learned about scarcity, opportunity cost, incentives, and supply and demand. This allowed me to understand why engineers always work with limited budgets, materials, and time. Every design is a trade-off between cost, performance, safety, and schedule. This allowed me to see that engineers do not work in a vacuum, free of constraints, but that for a design to be made, it not only has to be successful but also profitable for the company. A design that is too expensive, or that people have no incentive to adopt, will often lose out to a simpler one that is affordable and sustainable.

In conclusion, my general education courses at Iowa State really opened my eyes to the impacts my future work will have on people and improved my understanding of the role of an engineer. Music showed me that innovation can reshape culture far beyond its original purpose, history showed me that the decisions of the past can still affect communities today, and economics showed me that every solution involves trade-offs and costs. As an engineer, solving problems takes more than technical skills, because the economic, social, and global impact of a solution matters just as much as whether it works. Moving forward in my career, I plan to not only provide solutions to problems but ask myself, "Who does this help, who could it harm, and what will it cost over time?" Thanks to my general education courses, I feel prepared to design solutions that are not only effective but also responsible and fair.

What cybersecurity means to me

Ethics paper, CybE 234: Legal, Professional, and Ethical Issues in Cyber Systems

Cybersecurity is a complex field that holds many different meanings depending on the context. For me cybersecurity is a career path, as well as a need for everyone in our digital world, and lastly a reflection of my personal values and experiences. My introduction to cybersecurity started when my dad first introduced me to it around my junior year in high school, my dad being in information technology sales thought cybersecurity was a very up and coming field of study as he is surrounded by it on a day-to-day basis. After being introduced to the field by him, I still had little knowledge of what cybersecurity fully entailed. However, as I explored the subject, I became very interested in the ways security professionals could protect individuals, businesses, and governments from cyber threats. As I have continued in my pursuit of a career in Cyber security, I have realized how essential cybersecurity is to people who have access to the internet and I have seen firsthand the consequences of weak security practices and lack of knowledge of protecting your personal information. As I have grown in my career, I realized how much the people in my personal life have been affected by a lack of privacy online.

One of the most interesting aspects of cybersecurity, in my opinion, is how computers talk to each other and how people can make them do complicated things. The way technology can be pushed beyond what most people think is possible really excites me. I feel as if I know a secret world that nobody else really knows or pays attention to. With that being said, I still think it is very important to educate people on common cyber-attacks that they may run into such as “phishing” and “malware”. Educating people to not click on links or applications you do not recognize and giving people the skills to identify these types of attacks is very important to help people make safer choices and eases your life as a cybersecurity engineer. Aside from my technical interest in Cybersecurity, my passion for cybersecurity is also driven by my nature as a protector. Growing up as an older brother that had a little sister it has always been my job to take care of the people I care about. This upbringing of learning how to protect and care for those that can't protect themselves has shaped me into someone who would be perfect for cybersecurity. I find joy in bringing safety and comfort to others which is what I believe cybersecurity is all about at its core.

With all that being said cybersecurity is not always so sweet and fun. Beyond mastering the technical skills needed to monitor and secure networks, I have realized through my couple of weeks taking this ethics class that there are a lot of complex decisions you might face as you are out in the workforce of this field. One case that we studied in class being about Peiter C. Zatko also known as “mudge” who is known for being head of security at Twitter who aired out that Twitter's systems were insanely unsecure, and that Twitter would not let him fix it. In doing this he was labeled a whistleblower and was fired from Twitter. This had to have been an insanely difficult position for him as he basically had to choose between keeping quiet and putting all of Twitter’s users at risk and being fired from his position at Twitter where he was definitely making very good money. There are many more examples of cybersecurity employees making difficult decisions in consideration of privacy, legality, and morality. Navigating these dilemmas will be one of the most difficult aspects of working in cybersecurity, but I welcome the challenge.

In cyber security there are many different character traits of a person that can make them successful in the cyber security field. For me, I think there are two that really set me apart from other people in my field of work. I believe my strongest attribute is my competitive nature and relentless drive to improve. Growing up playing sports (my favorite being wrestling) and continuing to be in competitive environments has taught me that success does not always come immediately. Instead, it requires time, effort, and consistency to achieve things that are worthwhile. Learning this from a young age I believe is a big part of my success up until this point and will continue to be a pushing factor in my story yet to come. This mindset is crucial in cybersecurity because of the need to learn and adapt to stay ahead of evolving threats. This constant need for adaptation to stay ahead of threats allows me to view cybersecurity as a game/sport. Thinking about cybersecurity this way makes me want to learn the skills to beat hackers. It turns cybersecurity into a challenge that I’m determined to win.

One real-life experience that solidified my interest in cybersecurity was my grandmother telling me about her struggles with identity and credit card theft due to lack of security on the internet. Over this last thanksgiving break my grandma shared to me that she had her credit card and personal info stolen multiple times and just had to cancel all her credit cards except for her debit card due to this. She explained to me how much stress this put her through and how glad she is I am pursuing this field of work. Seeing how easily theft can happen and it affecting one of my loved ones made me realize how important cybersecurity is for everyone. This experience showed me why teaching people about online safety matters and that cybersecurity is not just about protecting large corporations or government agencies. Cybersecurity is about individuals, especially those who may not fully understand the risks of the digital world and their families.

Cybersecurity is more than just a career for me. It is a passion discovered through my experiences, rooted in my personal values, and want to help others. The field presents so many challenges and opportunities for me. With my competitive spirit and dedication to improvement, I am excited to develop the skills necessary to succeed in this field of work. Whether it is protecting individuals like my grandmother or securing large-scale systems, I am excited about the impact I can make in the world of cybersecurity.

Colten Stevens, Cyber Security Engineering, Iowa State University
